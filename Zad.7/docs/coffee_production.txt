Coffee production begins on coffee farms located in the tropical region between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, often called the coffee belt. Coffee plants need warm temperatures, plenty of rain and rich soil. Arabica coffee grows best at high altitudes, while Robusta coffee grows at lower altitudes and is more resistant to disease.

A coffee plant needs three to four years to produce its first fruit. The fruit is called a coffee cherry. When the cherries are ripe they turn bright red. In many countries the cherries are picked by hand, because they do not all ripen at the same time. In Brazil, where farms are flat and large, machines are often used for harvesting.

After harvesting, the cherries must be processed to remove the beans from the fruit. In the dry process, also called the natural process, the whole cherries are spread out in the sun to dry for several weeks. In the wet process, also called the washed process, the fruit pulp is removed by a machine, and the beans are fermented in water tanks and then dried. The washed process usually gives a cleaner and more acidic taste.

The dried beans are called green coffee. They are sorted, packed in jute bags and exported to roasters around the world. Green coffee can be stored for many months.

Roasting turns green coffee into the brown beans we know. During roasting the beans are heated to about 200 degrees Celsius. Sugars and acids inside the beans react and create hundreds of aroma compounds. A light roast keeps more of the original fruit flavors, while a dark roast tastes more bitter and smoky. After roasting, coffee should be ground just before brewing, because ground coffee loses its aroma quickly.
