The history of coffee begins in the highlands of Ethiopia, where the coffee plant Coffea arabica grows wild in the forests. According to a popular legend, a goat herder named Kaldi noticed that his goats became very energetic after eating red berries from a certain shrub. Whether or not the story is true, Ethiopia is widely considered the birthplace of coffee.

From Ethiopia, coffee spread across the Red Sea to Yemen in the 15th century. Sufi monks in Yemen drank coffee to stay awake during long night prayers. The port city of Mocha became the most important center of the coffee trade, and its name is still used today for some coffee drinks.

By the 16th century coffee had reached Persia, Egypt, Syria and the Ottoman Empire. Coffee houses opened in cities such as Istanbul and Cairo. They became places where people met to talk, listen to music, play chess and discuss politics.

In the 17th century coffee arrived in Europe through Venice. The first coffee houses in England were opened in Oxford in 1650 and in London in 1652. Because people exchanged news and ideas there, English coffee houses were sometimes called "penny universities", since a cup of coffee cost one penny.

The Dutch were the first Europeans to grow coffee on a large scale outside Arabia. They started plantations on the island of Java in Indonesia at the end of the 17th century. Later the French brought coffee plants to the Caribbean, and from there coffee spread to Central and South America.

Today Brazil is the largest coffee producer in the world, followed by Vietnam, Colombia, Indonesia and Ethiopia. Brazil alone produces about one third of the world's coffee.
